What CCTV inspection actually is

CCTV inspection means running a closed-circuit camera through a pipeline and recording continuous video of the interior wall. On larger mainlines that camera rides a motorized, self-leveling crawler; on service laterals and residential lines it is a push camera on a flexible rod. Either way, the operator logs distance as the camera travels, so every observation is tied to a footage mark you can find again later with a locator.

The video by itself is not the product. The product is the coded assessment. Under NASSCO’s Pipeline Assessment Certification Program (PACP), each observation gets a standardized code — a longitudinal crack, a root intrusion, a sag holding water, a tap installed at the wrong angle — along with a severity grade. That coding is what makes a report portable. An engineer in another office, a municipal reviewer, or a buyer’s attorney can read it without having to trust our adjectives.

When you need it

Before you buy

A pre-purchase sewer scope on a house with mature trees or clay pipe is the cheapest contingency in the transaction. Root intrusion and offset joints do not show up in a standard home inspection, and a full lateral replacement is a five-figure surprise after closing.

Before you accept new construction

On a residential development, a post-construction inspection catches debris left in the line, crushed sections from heavy equipment, and improperly seated joints while the subcontractor is still on the hook. Once the street is paved and the bond is released, that same defect is yours.

When something is wrong and nobody knows why

Recurring backups, unexplained infiltration, a line that jets clean and re-blocks in a month — these are diagnostic problems. Cleaning without inspecting treats the symptom. Inspecting first tells you whether you are dealing with roots, grease, a structural failure, or a bellied section that will never drain properly regardless of how often you clean it.

Conduit and low-voltage work

The same equipment verifies fiber optic and low-voltage conduit before cable is pulled. Finding a collapsed or obstructed duct before the pull is inconvenient. Finding it during the pull, with a crew and a reel on site, is expensive.

What the deliverable looks like

  • Continuous video of each run, delivered digitally.
  • PACP-coded observation log — every defect with its code, severity grade, and footage location.
  • Still images at each significant observation.
  • Line summary with material, diameter, condition rating, and access points used.
  • Plain-language findings so a non-engineer reading the report knows what to do next.

We can provide a redacted sample report on request so you know exactly what you are buying before you book.

How the visit runs

We need access — a cleanout, a manhole, or a pulled toilet on a residential lateral — and we need to know roughly where the line goes. Heavy debris or standing water blocks the camera’s view, so some lines need hydro-jetting before an inspection is worth doing; we will tell you if that is the case rather than handing you video of mud. If the problem is water entering the system rather than something obstructing it, smoke and dye testing is usually the better first move.

What we do not do

QPI does not inspect oil and gas pipelines or municipal wastewater collection systems. Our work is fiber optics, low-voltage conduit, residential utility prep, and private commercial and industrial lines. Being clear about that is not modesty — it means the crew that shows up has done your specific kind of job before.
